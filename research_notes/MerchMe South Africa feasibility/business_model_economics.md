# MerchMe: Business Model, Profit-Split Benchmarks and Unit Economics (ZAR)

Research date: 22 October 2026. FX used: **R16.3 per US$** (rand mid-rate about 0.0613 USD per rand on 28 Sep 2026; see Q4). That is stronger than the R17–19 assumed in the brief, so the model should be sensitivity-tested at R16–R18. "SA" means South African data. "Global" means US, UK or AU benchmarks. **[EST]** marks my own estimates or derivations, not sourced facts.

## 1. Merch licensing and music merch benchmarks: is a 25%-of-profit creator share "in line"?

### Takeaway
The claim does not hold up as worded. In music merch, the party that brings the audience normally keeps most of the profit. Tour merchandisers typically pay the artist **75–80% of net profit**, the exact inverse of MerchMe's 75/25, and D2C web-store royalties run **25–35% of retail price**. Licensing royalties (about 8–10% of wholesale or net sales) are lower, but the licensee in those deals carries inventory, manufacturing, distribution and marketing risk, and the licensor brings an established brand. On my SA unit economics, 25% of profit works out to only **about 12% of the VAT-inclusive retail price on a tee and about 6.5% on a hoodie [EST]**. That is roughly TeePublic or Redbubble territory, well below D2C music-merch norms.

### Cited Findings
**Licensing royalty rates (global, % of wholesale or net sales)**
- The Licensing Letter's survey put the average US/Canada consumer-products royalty at about **8.87% in 2019**, the highest since 2004 (9.4%). It has stayed in a **8.6–8.8%** band since 2005. Entertainment/character brands averaged **10.14%** in 2017 — [The Licensing Letter](https://www.thelicensingletter.com/tag/tlls-annual-licensing-business-survey); [TLL](https://www.thelicensingletter.com/?p=1827)
- LIMA (now Licensing International) global survey: the average royalty rose to **8.5% from 8.2%** in 2015 — [Licensing International](https://licensinginternational.org/news/global-retail-sales-of-licensed-goods-hit-251-7b-in-2015/)
- I found no current (2024–2026) Licensing International royalty-rate survey (see Gaps).

**Music merch deal structures (global, mostly US)**
- **Tour merchandising:** royalties are "usually based on sharing the net profits", where net profit = gross sales minus costs (manufacturing, card fees, freight, sales tax/VAT, hall fees). **"The artist or band typically receives 75-80% of the Net Profit."** — [Exploration.io, Merchandise Economics](https://exploration.io/merchandise-economics/) (2021, updated 2022)
- An industry guide gives a typical US artist royalty from a merchandiser of **30–40% of gross sales** (gross = retail minus taxes and card fees), with more for superstars and escalators after sales thresholds — [Number Analytics](https://www.numberanalytics.com/blog/ultimate-guide-merchandising-agreements-music). Secondary blog; treat as indicative.
- **D2C / web-store merch licensed to a merchandiser:** "U.S. royalty rates on average are in the range of **25% to 35%**", applied to the **retail price** rather than wholesale — [Exploration.io](https://exploration.io/merchandise-economics/)
- **Retail merchandising** (Hot Topic, Target, etc.): the artist earns a royalty on the **wholesale price**, not a profit share — [Exploration.io](https://exploration.io/merchandise-economics/). The brief's "8–15% of wholesale" range is consistent with the general licensing data above. Another guide cites **8–15% of net sales** for licensed product lines — [Number Analytics](https://www.numberanalytics.com/blog/ultimate-guide-merchandising-agreements-music)
- **Sublicensing:** a merch company typically keeps **15–25%** of sublicensed income and passes the rest to the artist — [Exploration.io](https://exploration.io/merchandise-economics/)
- Passman's *All You Need to Know About the Music Business* divides merch rights into three "flavors": tour, retail and D2C — [Scribd excerpt](https://www.scribd.com/document/821154100/All-You-Need-to-Know-About-the-Music-Business-PDF). A Bookey summary of the book says hall fees "usually range from 25% to 30% of gross sales" — [Bookey PDF summary](https://cdn.bookey.app/files/pdf/book/en/all-you-need-to-know-about-the-music-business-by-donald-s-passman.pdf). These are search-snippet excerpts; I could not read the full chapter.
- A blog claim that merch companies take "30% to 50% or more" of sales — [Vocal Media](https://vocal.media/fyi/the-hidden-goldmine-how-much-do-artists-earn-from-merchandising). Low-reliability source.

**Hall fees / venue merch cuts (global)**
- Hall fees typically **25–30% of gross** merch takings — [Exploration.io](https://exploration.io/merchandise-economics/)
- US venue merch cuts commonly **10–35% of gross**, arenas often **20–30%**, a few reports up to **45%**. Europe is historically lighter, and a growing list of small and mid-size venues waive the cut — [Oriol Guitart, "The Economics of Concert Merchandising", 3 Sep 2026](https://oriolguitart.com/blog/the-economics-of-concert-merchandising/)
- The most common split is **20/80** (venue keeps 20%). One venue (Ineffable Live) dropped its 20% fee, saving acts an estimated $1,000–$2,000 a night — [Decibel Magazine](https://decibelmagazine.com/2018/02/28/examining-merch-cuts/) (2018)
- Merchandisers often cap the hall fees they absorb (e.g. at 20%). Anything above the cap comes out of the artist's share — [Exploration.io](https://exploration.io/merchandise-economics/)

**360 deals (global)**
- Label merch share is usually **10–30% of merch revenue**: **20–30% of merch gross or 10–20% of merch net**. Some deals use a flat **15–25%** of all merch income — [Hypebot](https://www.hypebot.com/hypebot/2017/07/360-record-deals-a-look-inside-multiple-rights-agreements-part-2.html); [Musosoup](https://musosoup.com/blog/what-is-a-360-deal); [Mogul](https://www.usemogul.com/post/what-is-a-360-deal-in-music)
- An illustrative 360/merch clause pays the artist **22% of Net Retail Receipts for t-shirts, 20% for hoodies/sweatshirts, 15% for headwear**, **25% of Net Receipts on direct sales** and **60% of Net Licensing Receipts** — [Hypebot](https://www.hypebot.com/hypebot/2017/07/360-record-deals-a-look-inside-multiple-rights-agreements-part-2.html) (sample clause, not a published contract)

**Major music merch companies (global)**
- Bravado (UMG) has direct deals with **150+ artists**, and its business is split roughly **50/50 between live and retail/online** — [Music Week](https://www.musicweek.com/brands/read/bravado-merch-isn-t-just-about-t-shirts-and-mugs-anymore/065405). Bravado acquired licensing firm Epic Rights — [Music Week](https://musicweek.com/labels/read/umg-s-bravado-acquires-epic-rights/075064)
- LIMA put music merch sales at **$3.1bn in 2016** (+10%). Panic! At the Disco's manager said **30% of the band's tour profit** came from merch — [Rolling Stone, Feb 2018](https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-features/inside-musics-merch-gold-rush-199554/)
- No published Bravado, Merch Traffic (Sony), EMP/Probity (Warner), Live Nation/Fanatics or Epic Rights split was found — [Hrbek Law](https://www.hrbeklaw.com/resources/articles/merchandise-deals/) says only that the key variable is whether the share applies to gross or net (see Gaps)

### Inferences
- **The "in line with licensing" justification uses the wrong base.** Licensing royalties are a % of the licensee's *wholesale or net sales* and compensate for the licensee's capital, inventory risk, production and distribution. MerchMe is print-on-demand (no inventory risk), and its stated model relies on the *creator* to drive the traffic. In the music merch structure closest to MerchMe (D2C web store run by a merchandiser), the artist gets **25–35% of retail**, not 25% of profit.
- **Converted to like-for-like bases [EST]** (base case in Q4: R450 tee, R699 hoodie):
  - **MerchMe 25% of profit:** about R54 per tee (11.9% of VAT-incl retail, 13.7% of ex-VAT revenue) and about R46 per hoodie (6.5% of retail).
  - **D2C royalty of 25–35% of ex-VAT retail:** about R98–R137 per tee and R152–R213 per hoodie.
  - **Tour-style 75–80% of net profit:** about R161–R172 per tee.
  - **Retail licensing at 8–10% of wholesale:** with wholesale at roughly half of retail, that is only about 4–5% of retail. This is the one benchmark MerchMe beats, but it is the least comparable structure.
- MerchMe could defend the claim only if it supplies the demand (paid marketing, marketplace traffic, design, inventory risk), as a licensee does. If creators bring their own fans, the norm runs the other way.
- **Reputational risk:** SA creators and managers who know tour or D2C deals will see 75/25 as inverted. Mercha.co.za, a label-run SA competitor (see Q2), is already in the market.

### Gaps
- No 2024–2026 Licensing International royalty survey was found. The latest data points are TLL 2019 (8.87%) and LIMA 2015 (8.5%).
- No published artist split was found for Bravado, Merch Traffic, EMP/Probity, Live Nation/Fanatics or Epic Rights. These are private contracts.
- I could not read Passman's merch chapter directly. Rates attributed to Passman come from summaries and snippets.
- No SA-specific data on venue merch cuts or SA merchandiser deals was found.

## 2. Creator-platform benchmarks: what creators keep

### Takeaway
Every major creator-storefront platform (Fourthwall, Spring, Bonfire, Dashery, Printful+Shopify) uses the same model: a published **base cost**, a **creator-set price**, and **100% of the markup to the creator**. The platform's margin is built into the base cost. Marketplaces that supply their own traffic pay far less: **TeePublic pays a fixed $4 on a $23 tee (about 17% of retail)**, and Redbubble artists keep a default 10–20% markup, minus **20–50% account fees** unless they are top-1% Pro. MerchMe's 75/25 resembles the marketplace end of this range in creator take, while offering the storefront model, where creators bring their own demand.

## 3. How to define "profit", the fairness risk, and alternative model structures

### Takeaway
"Profit" must be contractually defined, capped and auditable. The difference between gross and net, and which costs are deductible, "can dramatically affect take-home income". The biggest risk is that creators cannot see or influence the deductions, and that 25% of a shrinking number feels worse than a fixed or percentage-of-revenue royalty. Market norms (storefront platforms, Mercha-style drops, D2C royalties) point MerchMe towards one of three things: transparent base cost plus creator markup, a %-of-revenue royalty, or a tiered profit share starting much higher than 25%.

### Cited Findings
- Merch deal splits hinge on gross vs net. Net subtracts "manufacturing, shipping, staff, venue fees, and payment processing fees or chargebacks". Agreements "should clearly define which expenses are deductible and include reporting requirements", and caps on certain expenses prevent deductions "from significantly reducing your share" — [Hrbek Law, Merchandise Deals (Jun 2026)](https://www.hrbeklaw.com/resources/articles/merchandise-deals/)
- Tour merch net profit = gross sales minus manufacturing, card fees, freight, sales tax/VAT and hall fees. The artist gets 75–80% of that — [Exploration.io](https://exploration.io/merchandise-economics/)
- Industry guides describe "gross sales" in merch royalty deals as retail minus taxes and card fees, and recommend **escalators** that raise the percentage after set sales thresholds — [Number Analytics](https://www.numberanalytics.com/blog/ultimate-guide-merchandising-agreements-music)
- Royalty tiers by product: 22% tees, 20% hoodies, 15% headwear (net retail receipts); 25% direct sales; 60% licensing — [Hypebot](https://www.hypebot.com/hypebot/2017/07/360-record-deals-a-look-inside-multiple-rights-agreements-part-2.html)
- **Creator-set markup** is the norm on Fourthwall, Spring, Bonfire and Dashery (see Q2) — [Fourthwall](https://fourthwall.com/pricing); [Spring FAQ](https://kb.spri.ng/creators/articles/frequently-asked-questions.html); [Dashery](https://dashery.com/)
- **Fixed royalty per item:** TeePublic $4 regular / $2 sale on a tee — [TeePublic](https://www.teepublic.com/designer-signup)
- **Tiered / performance-based:** Redbubble's Pro tier (top 1%) pays no platform fee, Premium pays 20% and Standard 50%. This is a tiering, though it penalises rather than rewards most creators — [Redbubble blog](https://blog.redbubble.com/2025/08/artist-account-tiers-and-fees/)
- **Campaign / pre-order drops:** Bonfire's base cost falls as campaign volume rises — [TopBubbleIndex](https://www.topbubbleindex.com/blog/bonfire-shirt-costs/). Mercha runs pre-order drops at about 10% off list (R699 → R629 hoodie) — [Mercha](https://mercha.co.za/)
- **Drop velocity (low reliability):** an aggregator claims 68% of limited-drop volume sells in the first 12 hours — [VoxBooster (Aug 2026)](https://voxbooster.com/pl/blog/merch-sales-creator-statistics-2026/). The sourcing is vague and should be treated as unverified.
- **Managed / merch-company tier:** traditional merchandisers provide advances, design and production, but advances are "returnable or recoupable", sometimes with interest, and tied to performance minimums — [Exploration.io](https://exploration.io/merchandise-economics/)

### Inferences
- **Recommended definition if profit share is kept [EST]:**
  - Net Profit = gross receipts (incl. shipping charged) minus VAT minus actual payment fees minus actual garment+print+packaging cost (published per SKU) minus actual outbound shipping minus refunds/reprints at cost.
  - Exclude, or cap at a stated % of revenue: MerchMe overhead, platform/tech costs and marketing. Include any marketing only with creator pre-approval.
  - Add monthly statements, audit rights and a published base-cost table.
- **Alternative structures to model [EST], per R450 tee (see Q4 for costs):**
  1. **Creator sets markup over a published base.** Example: a MerchMe base of R250 ex VAT (COGS about R160 plus a MerchMe margin of about R90). At R450 retail the creator nets about R125 (391.30 − 16.40 − 250). MerchMe keeps R90 plus control of the base. This matches market norms and lets creators price up for superfans.
  2. **% of revenue royalty:** 20–30% of ex-VAT revenue = R78–R117 per tee. Simple and audit-free, aligned with D2C royalties (25–35% of retail).
  3. **Tiered profit share:** for example 40% of net profit up to X units a year, 50% thereafter, and 60–75% for creators who drive paid campaigns. This mirrors merch escalators.
  4. **Fixed royalty per item:** for example R60–R80 per tee and R90–R120 per hoodie, similar to TeePublic's $4 (≈R65), with a lower "sale" rate.
  5. **Pre-order / campaign drops:** batch orders allow screen print or bulk DTF and lower blank costs. This raises the profit pool and suits launch moments.
  6. **Managed-service tier:** MerchMe designs, shoots and markets. A 50/50 or MerchMe-majority split, or a fee, is defensible here because MerchMe supplies the demand-generation work a licensee would.
- Keeping 75/25 is most defensible **only for the managed tier, where MerchMe does design, marketing and paid acquisition**. For self-serve "upload and share your link" creators, the market comparison favours the creator.

### Gaps
- No data was found on creator satisfaction or churn under profit-share vs markup models.
- No SA case law or industry standard on merch "net profit" definitions was found.

## 4. SA unit economics inputs for a POD tee and hoodie (ZAR)

### Takeaway
On current SA prices (Oct 2026), a **R450 tee** leaves about **R215** of "profit" and a **R699 hoodie** about **R182**, after VAT, payment fees, blank, DTF print, packaging and a reprint/returns reserve. These figures exclude shipping (assumed passed to the customer at cost), marketing and overhead **[EST]**. Small-quantity hoodie blanks (about R290–R352 ex VAT) make hoodies less profitable per unit than tees at the prices Mercha charges. The main cost levers are blank sourcing at volume, DTF gang-sheet efficiency, and whether shipping is subsidised.

## 5. Customer acquisition: creator-driven traffic economics

### Takeaway
Hard, published benchmarks for follower-to-buyer conversion are scarce. The most-cited range (**0.4–1.2% of followers buy merch**) comes from a low-reliability aggregator. Concert data is solid: about 1 in 4 attendees buy, at about $45 per buyer. POD marketplace data shows an extreme long tail: Redbubble paid out about **A$112 per selling artist per year (FY22) [EST]**. MerchMe's revenue will be concentrated in a few creators, so the model should assume most shops sell little or nothing.

### Cited Findings
- **Follower conversion:** "0.40% to 1.20%" of social followers buy physical merch. Average order value **$48.50**. **28%** of merch buyers make repeat purchases within a year. Hoodies make up 38% of creator merch units, tees 31%, accessories 18%. 78% of creator stores use POD. Average creator gross margin on POD 42%. Sourcing is attributed vaguely to Fourthwall, Spring, Shopify and Linktree — [VoxBooster, Aug 2026](https://voxbooster.com/pl/blog/merch-sales-creator-statistics-2026/). **Low reliability**: a software vendor's blog with no primary links, so use only as a sensitivity range.
- **Concert merch (atVenu, US):** about **$10 per attendee**, about **1 in 4** attendees buy, about **$45 per buyer** (up to $90+ at stadiums), **2.1 items per purchase** (up from 1.7). The average act grossed **$10,000+** in merch per concert in 2023. Eras Tour merch about **$40 per attendee**, **$440m+** in total — [Oriol Guitart, Sep 2026](https://oriolguitart.com/blog/the-economics-of-concert-merchandising/)
- **Redbubble long tail:**
  - FY22: **809k selling artists**, against artist revenue of **A$90.8m** — [Wikipedia, citing FY22 annual report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redbubble); [ASX FY22 Appendix 4E](https://announcements.asx.com.au/asxpdf/20220817/pdf/45cxtlwtz0qbpl.pdf)
  - FY23: 5.0M customers bought 4.8M designs from 650K artists — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redbubble)
  - March 2014: 51,900 artists had "successfully sold" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redbubble)
- **Dashery (creator-traffic storefronts):** A$4.0m gross processed sales across **1,500+ active selling accounts** in FY26 — [SmallCaps](https://smallcaps.com.au/article/articore-group-returns-to-profitability-in-fy26-as-gross-margin-hits-record)
- Fourthwall reports 500,000+ creators and companies using the platform — [Fourthwall](https://fourthwall.com/pricing)
- Fourthwall says sending a personal thank-you note "increases sales by over 30%" — [Fourthwall](https://fourthwall.com/pricing) (vendor claim)

### Inferences
- **Average earnings per artist [EST]:**
  - Redbubble FY22: A$90.8m ÷ 809k selling artists ≈ **A$112 per selling artist per year**.
  - Dashery FY26: A$4.0m ÷ 1,500+ accounts ≈ **A$2,700 GPS per active account per year** at most.
  - Creator-traffic storefronts produce far more per account than open marketplaces, but averages hide a power law.
- **Drop sizing heuristic [EST]:** a creator with 20,000 engaged followers at 0.4–1.2% conversion gives roughly **80–240 buyers per year** (not per drop). At 1.3 units per order and R450, that is roughly R47k–R140k of annual GMV per such creator. This is highly uncertain. Validate with pilot drops.
- **AOV:** the global $48.50 (≈R790) is likely above SA levels. Mercha prices imply an SA AOV of about R450–R900 for one or two items [EST].
- Repeat buying (about 28%, unverified) suggests value in collecting buyer data. Contract terms on data ownership matter (see Q7).
- MerchMe should model the share of creators who make zero sales explicitly; I found no published share for any platform (see Gaps). In model scenarios, assume most sign-ups produce negligible GMV and the top 5–10% produce the bulk [EST].

### Gaps
- No primary-source data on the share of creators who ever make a sale on Spring, Fourthwall or Redbubble.
- No credible published follower-to-buyer conversion study. The 0.4–1.2% figure is unverified.
- No SA creator merch conversion, AOV or repeat-rate data.
- No published "drop" sales-curve data from a primary source.

## 6. Platform economics: realistic take rates, gross margins and failure modes

### Takeaway
The only listed pure-play POD marketplace, **Articore (Redbubble/TeePublic)**, made a **49.6% gross margin** on marketplace revenue but only a **28.6% margin after paid acquisition** in FY26. It returned to a full-year profit of just **A$10.9m** on **A$354.5m** of marketplace revenue, while revenue shrank 6.5%. It pays artists only about **16% of total revenue**, and it raised artist fees in 2025. Its creator-storefront unit, Dashery, earns a higher **36.5% GPAPA margin** because creators bring their own demand. The lesson for MerchMe: margins are thin once you pay for traffic, so the creator-traffic model is the right one, but in that model the creator is the scarce asset.

### Cited Findings
**Articore FY26 (year to 30 Jun 2026, A$)**
- Marketplace revenue (MPR) **A$354.5m (−6.5%)**. Total revenue A$408.5m (−6.9%).
- Gross profit **A$175.9m (+1.7%)**, **49.6% margin** (record).
- **GPAPA A$101.4m, 28.6% margin** (+210bp), "reflecting supply-chain efficiencies, pricing, improved paid marketing effectiveness and **changes to artist account fees**".
- EBIT **A$10.3m**. Net profit **A$10.9m**, the first full-year profit since listing outside FY21.
- Opex **A$85.0m**, down from A$91.3m. Cash A$40.5m.
- FY27 guidance: GPAPA margin 27–30%.
- Sources: [SmallCaps, 27 Aug 2026](https://smallcaps.com.au/article/articore-group-returns-to-profitability-in-fy26-as-gross-margin-hits-record); [Inside Retail, Aug 2026](https://insideretail.com.au/business/articore-reports-revenue-decline-lifts-gross-profit-202608). The prior-year EBIT loss is reported as A$9.8m and the statutory net loss as A$11.3m, which are different line items.
- Redbubble's gross margin was 52.2% for the year and **55.2% in Q4**. TeePublic MPR grew 2.8% (constant currency) and its gross profit 10.9% — [SmallCaps](https://smallcaps.com.au/article/articore-group-returns-to-profitability-in-fy26-as-gross-margin-hits-record)

**Artist revenue vs marketplace revenue (Redbubble)**
- FY21: total revenue A$657.3m, artist revenue **A$104.0m**, MPR A$553.3m — [FY21 Annual Report](https://www.articore.com/FormBuilder/_Resource/_module/VFV_6YfHZ0qKveaD4XGZZA/files/presentations/FY21_Annual_Report.pdf)
- FY22: MPR A$482.6m, artist revenue **A$90.8m**, total A$573.4m — [ASX FY22](https://announcements.asx.com.au/asxpdf/20220817/pdf/45cxtlwtz0qbpl.pdf)
- FY23: artist revenue **A$87.6m** vs MPR A$467.5m — [ASX FY23](https://announcements.asx.com.au/asxpdf/20230822/pdf/05sw7n2y5pykds.pdf)

**Other platforms**
- Redbubble artist fees from Sep 2025: 50% (Standard), 20% (Premium), 0% (Pro, top 1%), capped at $150 per period — [Redbubble blog](https://blog.redbubble.com/2025/08/artist-account-tiers-and-fees/)
- Spring creators report payout problems that "have gotten worse through 2025 and into 2026" — [ecommerce-platforms.com](https://ecommerce-platforms.com/articles/best-teespring-alternatives)
- Fourthwall monetises through base-cost margins, 5% on digital products and memberships, and a $15/month Pro plan — [Fourthwall](https://fourthwall.com/pricing)

### Inferences
- **Derived Articore ratios [EST]:**
  - **Paid acquisition** ≈ A$175.9m − A$101.4m = **A$74.5m**, about **21% of MPR**.
  - **Opex** about **24% of MPR**.
  - **EBIT margin** about **2.9% of MPR**.
  - **Artist share of total revenue** ≈ **15.8%** in FY21, FY22 and FY23 alike (104.0/657.3; 90.8/573.4; 87.6/555.1).
- MerchMe's 75/25 gives creators about **13.7% of ex-VAT revenue per tee [EST]**, slightly below Redbubble's about 16%. Redbubble, however, *buys* traffic (about 21% of MPR), while MerchMe expects creators to bring it. Creators will compare MerchMe to storefront platforms, not to Redbubble.
- **Ceiling:** a global marketplace with millions of customers earns roughly a 3% EBIT margin. A local SA platform's upside depends on low acquisition cost (creator-driven traffic), local fulfilment speed and price, and a few high-volume creators. Dashery's 36.5% GPAPA margin vs Redbubble's 28.6% supports the creator-traffic thesis.
- **Failure modes:**
  1. Creators churn to storefront platforms or local competitors (Mercha) if the split is seen as unfair.
  2. A long tail of zero-sales shops consumes support and onboarding cost.
  3. Shipping subsidies eat the margin.
  4. Payout reliability problems destroy trust (the Spring example).
  5. Fee hikes on creators to fix margins create backlash (Redbubble 2025).
  6. Paid marketing becomes necessary, pushing margins towards Articore's thin levels.

### Gaps
- No financials found for Printful, Spring/Amaze or Fourthwall (all private). Fourthwall funding data is from 2021.
- Articore's exact definition of how artist payments sit in FY26 cost of sales was not verified from the FY26 annual report. Ratios use FY21–FY23 disclosures.
- No SA POD platform financials were found (Mercha, TeePrint).

## 7. Contract and IP structure for a profit-share deal

### Takeaway
Market practice for POD and creator platforms is a **non-exclusive** licence of the creator's designs, a **creator-owned brand and data**, **monthly payouts** with a low threshold, and **no lock-in**. Traditional merch company deals add exclusivity, recoupable advances, performance minimums and sell-off rights. MerchMe should copy the platform norms for self-serve creators and use merchandiser-style terms only for a managed tier.

### Cited Findings
- Merch agreements should give the artist approval rights over designs and product categories, and make clear who owns design files (logos, artwork). They should also address quality control and sample approval, responsibility for defective goods and fan returns, who runs the online store, **ownership of customer data**, payment schedules and settlement timelines, termination (including performance-based early termination), and post-termination ownership of inventory, data and files, with buyback or sell-through periods — [Hrbek Law, Jun 2026](https://www.hrbeklaw.com/resources/articles/merchandise-deals/)
- Merch company deals require the artist to license "your name and likeness". They often include sublicensing rights (the merch company keeps 15–25% of sublicensed income). Tour advances are typically recoupable or returnable, sometimes with interest, and conditional on starting the tour within about **3 months** and meeting **performance minimums** — [Exploration.io](https://exploration.io/merchandise-economics/)
- **Redbubble payouts:** **$20/£20/€20 threshold**. Earnings below it roll over. Payments begin on the **15th of the month** — [Redbubble Help](https://help.redbubble.com/hc/articles/360027407652). Redbubble's marketplace is non-exclusive, with artists setting their own margins — [Redbubble blog](https://blog.redbubble.com/2025/08/artist-account-tiers-and-fees/)
- **TeePublic** pays on the 15th of the following month — [AutoKeyWorder](https://autokeyworder.com/blog/teepublic-vs-redbubble/)
- **Fourthwall:** no contracts, monthly automatic payouts with no fees. "You own your data and brand. We'll never send marketing to your supporters or sell their information." Your art is not used to train AI models — [Fourthwall](https://fourthwall.com/pricing)
- **Amazon Merch on Demand** is explicitly **non-exclusive** — [Amazon Merch](https://merch.amazon.com/)
- **PayFast** charges **R8.70 per payout** (ex VAT) — [PayFast](https://payfast.io/fees). **Paystack** charges R3 per transfer — [Paystack](https://support.paystack.com/en/articles/2130306). Both are relevant to setting MerchMe's payout threshold and frequency.

### Inferences
**Recommended self-serve terms [EST]:**
- A non-exclusive, revocable, royalty-bearing licence to reproduce the creator's designs on MerchMe products only, for the term plus a short sell-off period for open orders.
- The creator retains copyright and trademark. MerchMe gets a limited licence to use the creator's name and likeness for marketing the shop.
- Creator warranties of originality and IP clearance (samples, logos, fan art), with a takedown process.
- Monthly payouts above a threshold of about R100–R200, so that R8.70 payout fees and admin stay below about 5–10% of a small payout.
- Statements showing each deduction, audit rights, and a definition of "profit" with a published cost table (see Q3).
- Termination on 30 days' notice. Customer data shared with, or co-owned by, the creator (POPIA consent required).

**Managed tier [EST]:** MerchMe supplies design, marketing and possibly advances. This justifies a longer term (12–24 months), optional exclusivity for the merch category, recoupable advances and a MerchMe-majority split. Even here, music norms (artist 75–80% of tour net profit; 25–35% of retail for D2C) set expectations.

**Contract-structure caution [EST]:** if MerchMe is the merchant of record, it carries VAT, consumer-protection obligations (returns) and chargeback liability, and the creator receives a royalty. This allocation should be explicit. SA legal and tax specifics (POPIA, CPA returns, VAT on royalties) are for the legal workstream to verify.

### Gaps
- Redbubble's and Fourthwall's full licence clauses (scope, term, sublicensing) were not read directly.
- Mercha's creator contract terms are not public.
- SA-specific legal requirements (CPA cooling-off and returns, POPIA data sharing, VAT treatment of creator royalties paid to non-VAT-registered individuals) were not researched here and need legal verification.
