# Flip the Split, Then Prove MerchMe's Demand

MerchMe can work in South Africa, but not as pitched. The partners need to drop the 75/25 split and accept that they would be joining an existing category, not creating one. A Randburg start-up, Mercha, already runs almost exactly this model: no upfront cost, local DTF/DTG printing, R399 tees, 3–5 day delivery, and Nasty C and A-Reece on its roster. The difference is that Mercha reportedly pays artists **75% of profit and keeps 25%**, the exact inverse of MerchMe's offer ([Hype Magazine](https://hypemagazine.co.za/2024/12/18/mercha-turns-artist-recognition-into-revenue/)). The claim that 75/25 is "in line with merch licensing deals" also fails. Licensing royalties are low because the licensee carries inventory, production and marketing. In the music structures closest to MerchMe, artists keep **75–80% of net profit** on tour merch, or **25–35% of retail** on web-store merch ([Exploration.io](https://exploration.io/merchandise-economics/)). The market is real but small. Inside R159bn of South African online retail ([Mastercard](https://www.mastercard.com/news/eemea/en/newsroom/press-releases/en/2026/august/sa-online-retail-to-reach-r159-billion-in-2026/)) sits an estimated R135–340m a year of creator and artist merch demand [EST]. Fans reach it through bio links, because South Africa has no TikTok Shop and no Instagram Shops. A creator-fair "base price plus markup" structure pays creators about R125 per R450 tee. Under it, MerchMe earns R50–R110 per item, depending on how much printing it brings in-house, so it needs about 2,300 orders a month (about 76 a day) to cover a lean team costing about R310k a month [EST]. In the base scenario, MerchMe reaches about 1,600 orders a month by month 36, has used about R5.1m and has still not broken even. Only the upside scenario breaks even, in month 26 with about R2.9m of peak funding [EST]. The partners should spend about R250k on a 10-week concierge pilot first [EST]. It needs to show that followers convert into buyers and that creators accept the terms, before the partners commit about R650k to a custom build [EST]. MerchMe should then compete on what Mercha lacks: instant self-serve shops for the long tail of TikTok and Instagram creators, a checkout built for South African payment methods, and weekly payouts in rand.

## Fan merch is an estimated R135–340m niche inside R159bn of online retail

The wallet MerchMe is chasing sits inside a fast-growing but narrow channel. South African online retail passed **R130bn in 2025** and is forecast at **R159bn in 2026 (+22.5%)**, the first full year at 10% of retail ([Mastercard](https://www.mastercard.com/news/eemea/en/newsroom/press-releases/en/2026/august/sa-online-retail-to-reach-r159-billion-in-2026/)). Clothing is the category the most online shoppers buy (about 36%) ([World Wide Worx 2026](https://www.mastercard.com/news/media/hwgncwio/online-retail-in-south-africa-2026.pdf)). Shein and Temu took an estimated R7.3bn in 2024, "nearly 40% of online sales in the category" ([World Wide Worx 2025](https://www.worldwideworx.com/onlineretailsa2025/)). That implies online clothing of about R18–19bn in 2024 and plausibly **R22–28bn in 2026** [EST]. Recent tax changes help a local player. Since September 2024 low-value imported parcels pay 15% VAT on top of the 20% flat duty ([Webber Wentzel](https://www.webberwentzel.com/News/Pages/sars-increases-customs-duties-for-online-retail-orders-what-you-need-to-know.aspx)), cross-border growth slowed to 7% in 2025 ([World Wide Worx 2026](https://www.mastercard.com/news/media/hwgncwio/online-retail-in-south-africa-2026.pdf)), and **46% of shoppers trust local platforms more, against 10% for overseas retailers** ([Mastercard 2025](https://www.mastercard.com/news/eemea/en/newsroom/press-releases/en/2025-1/september/south-africa-s-online-retail-set-to-surpass-r130-billion-in-2025)).

The audience is large on paper but much smaller once you count who actually buys. TikTok reaches **29.1m South African adults (+33% year on year)**, against Instagram's **8.6m** ([DataReportal 2026](https://datareportal.com/reports/digital-2026-south-africa)). About 18.6m people are aged 18–34 [EST, from DataReportal age bands], but only **34.2% of adults buy goods online**, with the peak at 39.5% among 25–34s ([World Wide Worx 2026](https://www.mastercard.com/news/media/hwgncwio/online-retail-in-south-africa-2026.pdf)). That gives roughly 7m online shoppers aged 18–34 [EST]. Buying is concentrated among the employed and better-off. Youth unemployment is **47.4%**, with only 5.6m of 15–34s in work ([Stats SA](https://www.statssa.gov.za/publications/P0211/Media%20Release%20QLFS%20Q2%202026.pdf)), and consumer confidence stands at **−13** ([BER](https://www.ber.ac.za/Documents/Index/FNBBER-Consumer-Confidence-Index)). A working pool of 4–5m young online buyers is therefore more realistic [EST]. Spending is modest: about 49% of online shoppers spent R400 or less online in the previous month [EST, summed from TGI bands in [World Wide Worx 2026](https://www.mastercard.com/news/media/hwgncwio/online-retail-in-south-africa-2026.pdf)]. For many fans, a R450 tee is a deliberate purchase.

The creator side is large and under-monetised, which is the strongest part of the case. Humanz counts **at least 330,000 registered South African creators**. About one in six actively pursued paid brand work last year, only about one in five has ever been paid in cash, **55% of paid creators earn under R5,000 a month**, and creators rate income predictability **2.1 out of 5** ([Humanz 2026](https://www.humanz.com/south-african-creator-economy-report-2026/)). TikTok's Creator Rewards programme is not available in South Africa ([TikTok Creator Academy](https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/en/article/creator-rewards-program)), so owned income streams matter more here than in markets where platforms pay creators directly. Musicians add a second segment. SAMRO has **28,308 members** ([SAMRO](https://www.samro.org.za/)), and **about 3,550 SA artists** were added to Spotify editorial playlists in 2025. SA artists earned **R504m on Spotify, 74% of it from listeners abroad** ([africa.com](https://africa.com/entertainment/south-african-artists-generated-over-zar-504m-on-spotify-in-2025)). PwC forecasts South African live music to grow at **5.7% a year, the fastest rate in the world** ([PwC](https://www.pwc.co.za/en/press-room/africa-entertainment-media-outlook.html)). That matters because gigs are where merch sells: about one in four US concert attendees buys merch ([Oriol Guitart](https://oriolguitart.com/blog/the-economics-of-concert-merchandising/)).

Price evidence is consistent across sellers. Tees cluster at **R350–R500** and hoodies at **R630–R900**: Mercha sells tees at R399–R499 and hoodies at R629–R699 ([Mercha](https://mercha.co.za)), GALXBOY's Scorpion Kings tees cost R400 ([scorpionkings.store](https://scorpionkings.store/)), and Zola 7's R350 delivered tee "sold like hotcakes" ([IOL](https://iol.co.za/lifestyle/style-beauty/fashion/2024-10-31-zola-7s-new-t-shirt-line-sparks-unprecedented-fan-support/)). At the top end, AKA's estate drew a "cash grab" backlash for R1,200–R2,000 pieces ([IOL](https://iol.co.za/lifestyle/style-beauty/fashion/2026-03-20-insane-r2k-price-tags-the-megacy-is-divided-as-premium-aka-tribute-collection-drops/)). No source measures South African fan spend on merch or how often followers turn into buyers. Every rand figure in the sizing below is therefore an explicit assumption, cross-checked from two directions.

The practical conclusion is that **break-even needs roughly 6–14% of the whole serviceable market** [EST]. That is achievable for a category leader, but Mercha is already competing for the same fans. The diaspora is a real extension: 74% of SA artists' Spotify royalties come from abroad. It is best served later through an overseas print partner rather than from the start.

## Mercha already sells MerchMe's promise, with the split reversed

The most important competitive fact is that MerchMe's headline proposition is already on sale. Hype Magazine's December 2024 profile of Mercha describes artists who bring their own designs earning **75% of profits**, or 65% with 10% to a Mercha designer. Mercha takes "a consistent 25% cut, but only after sales are made. No upfront costs, no minimum orders and no risk." It also runs in-house DTF, DTG and embroidery, pays profit share monthly once a creator's balance reaches **R500**, and offers live-event splits of 70/30, or 50/50 when Mercha absorbs production costs ([Hype Magazine](https://hypemagazine.co.za/2024/12/18/mercha-turns-artist-recognition-into-revenue/)). The team is label-linked: the same profile cites Universal Music and Trace Africa experience, and Mercha's own About Us page names the founders of record label Playground Productions ([Mercha About Us](https://mercha.co.za/about-us/)). By October 2026 the site runs on a WooCommerce multi-vendor store with **creator self-registration**. It hosts stores for Nasty C, A-Reece, Rowlene, Sliqe and DJ Knator, promises 3–5 day delivery, and carries a "NEW SITE, NEW ERA" banner ([Mercha](https://mercha.co.za)). The current site does not publish the split, so the partners should check Mercha's present terms by signing up as a creator. Taken at face value, though, a fan buying a tee generates an estimated **R129 for a Mercha creator on a R399 tee, against R54 for a MerchMe creator on a R450 tee under 75/25** [EST, applying MerchMe's cost assumptions to Mercha's price; Mercha's actual costs are unknown]. A creator choosing between the two would earn about 2.4 times more at Mercha.

Mercha's weaknesses are about speed and reach, not the offer itself. In 2024 its onboarding took about two weeks of design collaboration, then 3–5 days of "digital application" and two days of upload ([Hype Magazine](https://hypemagazine.co.za/2024/12/18/mercha-turns-artist-recognition-into-revenue/)). Its roster is curated, music-centric and Gauteng-heavy. Self-registration shows it is closing the speed gap, so MerchMe's differentiation window is real but short.

Other local players fill neighbouring niches. Streetwear brands act as merch partners for big acts, as GALXBOY did with eight R400 tee designs for the Scorpion Kings' FNB Stadium show ([scorpionkings.store](https://scorpionkings.store/)). Universal Music SA runs its own apparel store ([UMG Africa](https://umg.africa/collections/apparel)). Brandly sells a "creator merch" service, but with set-up fees of R999–R6,000 plus R150–R200 a month ([Brandly](https://brandly.co.za/)). The no-minimum printers (TeePrint, OTC Printing, Lucani, T-POD, Hopper SA) are suppliers rather than storefronts, and none offers a real order API ([Insaka](https://www.insaka.co.za/blog/print-on-demand-in-south-africa-local-vs-international-options)). Takealot's R400 monthly subscription, 5–18% success fees and warehoused stock do not suit zero-upfront print-on-demand ([EasySel](https://easysel.com/blog/takealot-seller-fees-and-profitability/)).

Global print-on-demand platforms are cheap for creators but expensive for South African fans. Fourthwall charges a published base cost, lets creators "keep 100% of profits" and ships to South Africa ([Fourthwall](https://fourthwall.com/pricing)). However, Printful charges **US$12.49 to ship one tee to South Africa, with delivery in 4–22 business days** ([Printful](https://www.printful.com/shipping)), and imported clothing attracts up to 45% duty plus 15% VAT ([HKTDC](https://research.hktdc.com/en/article/MTc5ODIxNjcyNA)). A US$25 tee therefore lands at roughly **R1,000–R1,130 against R399 locally** [EST, at R16.3–R18/US$]. Gelato lists South Africa as a production country but does not print apparel here ([Gelato](https://support.gelato.com/en/articles/8996105-where-is-my-apparel-clothing-order-printed)). Stripe is only "extended network" in South Africa, routed through Paystack ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/global)). Stan Store cannot offer Stripe Custom accounts here ([Stan](https://help.stan.store/article/217-countries-available-for-stripe-custom-accounts)), and Fourthwall pays non-Stripe countries monthly through bill.com ([Fourthwall Help](https://help.fourthwall.com/frequently-asked-questions/payments-and-pricing/country-not-supported-by-stripe)). Global platforms therefore matter mainly as the benchmark creators will use to judge fairness, not as a direct threat. Marketplaces pay far less: TeePublic pays **US$4 (≈R65) on a US$23 tee** ([TeePublic](https://www.teepublic.com/designer-signup)), and Redbubble Premium accounts have paid a 20% platform fee since September 2025 ([Redbubble](https://blog.redbubble.com/2025/08/artist-account-tiers-and-fees/)). But those marketplaces bring their own traffic, and MerchMe expects creators to bring theirs.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mercha | 75% of profit (≈R129 on R399 [EST]) | None | R399–R499, 3–5 days | Monthly, R500 minimum |

| GALXBOY-style retail partner | Negotiated, for big acts only | Varies | R400, in-store and online | Negotiated |

Native social commerce is absent, and that is why MerchMe's link-in-bio approach makes sense. TikTok Shop is not among its 24 live markets ([Digital Product Labs](https://dpl.company/countries-with-access-to-tiktok-shop-seller-center/)). South Africa is missing from Meta's supported countries for Shops and product tagging ([Instagram Help](https://help.instagram.com/321000045119159)). TikTok's new SA "One Creator Marketplace" covers brand deals, not commerce ([Business Day](https://www.businessday.co.za/companies/2026-10-01-tiktok-hopeful-about-new-monetisation-platform-for-local-creators-and-influencers/)). Every competitor relies on the same link out to a web store, so no one has a platform advantage.

In order of defensibility, MerchMe's genuine differentiators are these. First, **an upload-to-live mobile shop in minutes** for the long tail of TikTok and Instagram creators and emerging musicians, rather than a curated, weeks-long onboarding. Second, **transparent, creator-set pricing with weekly rand payouts and a low threshold**, against Mercha's monthly payouts above R500 and global platforms' USD payouts. Third, **a checkout built for South Africa**: Instant EFT, Capitec Pay and PayShap together now roughly equal cards as preferred methods ([World Wide Worx 2026](https://www.mastercard.com/news/media/hwgncwio/online-retail-in-south-africa-2026.pdf)). It also needs guest checkout and pickup-point delivery, and must load fast in TikTok and Instagram's in-app browsers. Fourth, **drop and gig tooling** such as pre-order countdowns, QR codes for live shows and per-channel analytics. Fifth, an **"official merch" position against bootlegs**: unofficial Uncle Waffles, Black Coffee and Tyla tees sell on Etsy, Redbubble and local Shopify stores ([Etsy](https://www.etsy.com/listing/1257231211/uncle-waffles-t-shirt); [4regn](https://4regn.com/products/graphic-tee-new-edition-uncle-waffles)), while Black Coffee's own shop still says "coming soon" ([Black Coffee](https://www.realblackcoffee.net/shop)). Zero upfront cost, local printing and 3–5 day delivery are table stakes, not differentiators, because Mercha already offers them. None of the five is a moat. Each is a head start that Mercha could copy within a year. That makes a partnership with a printer, or even with Mercha, a serious option alongside going head to head.

## The 75/25 split inverts every benchmark it claims to follow

The licensing comparison uses the wrong base and the wrong allocation of risk. Consumer-products licensing royalties averaged **8.87% of wholesale or net sales** in the latest survey found ([The Licensing Letter](https://www.thelicensingletter.com/tag/tlls-annual-licensing-business-survey)). The licensee in those deals funds inventory, manufacturing, distribution and marketing, and the licensor brings an established brand. MerchMe carries no inventory risk, because it prints on demand, and it expects creators to bring the demand. In music merch the party that brings the audience keeps most of the profit. On tour merch, "the artist or band typically receives **75–80% of the Net Profit**". On direct-to-consumer web-store deals, US royalties run **25–35% of retail price** ([Exploration.io](https://exploration.io/merchandise-economics/)). Even 360 record deals, the industry's most label-friendly structure, give the label only **10–30% of merch revenue** ([Hypebot](https://www.hypebot.com/hypebot/2017/07/360-record-deals-a-look-inside-multiple-rights-agreements-part-2.html)). The only public company in this business shows how thin margins are. Articore (Redbubble/TeePublic) made a **49.6% gross margin but only 28.6% after paid acquisition**, and a net profit of A$10.9m on A$354.5m of marketplace revenue. Its creator storefront unit, Dashery, earns more (36.5%) "since creators bring their own demand" ([SmallCaps](https://smallcaps.com.au/article/articore-group-returns-to-profitability-in-fy26-as-gross-margin-hits-record)). That is the right model for MerchMe, but in it the creator is the scarce asset, and creators can choose.

| Benchmark | Structure | Creator's take on a R450 tee | Who supplies demand and risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| MerchMe as pitched | 25% of profit | ≈R54, 11.9% of retail [EST] | Creator brings the fans |
| Tour merchandiser | 75–80% of net profit | ≈R161–R172 [EST] | Artist's live audience |
| D2C web-store licence | 25–35% of ex-VAT retail | ≈R98–R137 [EST] | Artist's audience; merchandiser runs the store |
| Storefront platforms (Fourthwall, Spring, Dashery) | 100% of markup over a published base | ~40–58% of retail at US prices | Creator; platform earns its margin inside the base |
| Marketplaces (TeePublic, Redbubble) | Fixed royalty or markup minus fees | ≈R65 (TeePublic); Redbubble ~16% of total revenue [EST] | Marketplace buys the traffic |
| Retail licensing | 8–10% of wholesale | ≈4–5% of retail [EST] | Licensee carries inventory and marketing |

Converted to like-for-like terms, 25% of profit is about **R54 on a R450 tee and R46 on a R699 hoodie** [EST]. The only benchmark 75/25 beats is retail licensing, the least comparable structure, and it lands at marketplace payout levels while asking creators to do the marketplace's job. A profit share also carries a fairness risk that creators and managers will notice. Under a profit split, the creator cannot see or influence the deductions. Merch lawyers advise defining exactly which expenses are deductible, capping them and requiring reporting ([Hrbek Law](https://www.hrbeklaw.com/resources/articles/merchandise-deals/)).

Four structures would hold up, each matched to how much work MerchMe does. The default for self-serve creators should be **Creator Standard**. MerchMe publishes a base price for each product, for example **R250 ex VAT for a tee and R480 ex VAT for a hoodie** [EST]. The creator sets the retail price and keeps everything above base after VAT and payment fees: **R124.90 on a R450 tee and R103.46 on a R699 hoodie** [EST], or about R145 on a hoodie priced at R749 [EST]. This is the norm on every storefront platform. It needs no audit, it lets creators price up for superfans, and it leaves MerchMe the gains when it cuts printing costs. A **MerchMe Pro** managed tier, in which MerchMe supplies design, a product shoot, paid ads and drop management, can defend a 50/50 split of net profit, because there MerchMe does a licensee's demand-generation work. **Live and event** pop-ups that MerchMe staffs should pay creators 70% of net profit, matching the live terms Mercha already offers. If the partners want to keep "split" language, the **fallback** is a 60/40 share of contractually defined net profit in the creator's favour, rising to 70/30 above 250 units in a rolling 12 months [EST]. The model shows this version breaking even later than base-plus-markup, because creators share in the savings from in-house printing.

The self-serve contract should follow platform norms rather than the norms of traditional merch companies. That means a non-exclusive licence of the designs, with the creator keeping copyright. Fan data is shared with the creator only under consent that complies with POPIA. Payouts go out weekly above about R100, with a holdback until the 7-day cooling-off period has passed. Creators get statements, and either side can terminate on 30 days' notice ([Hrbek Law](https://www.hrbeklaw.com/resources/articles/merchandise-deals/); [Fourthwall](https://fourthwall.com/pricing)) [EST on specific thresholds].

The Stage A costs are an assumption: an outsourced partner's margin added to the hub costs. They fall within the R150–R250 range implied for a single outsourced tee. Insaka's illustrative SA tee uses R200 for blank and print and leaves **R70 profit, about 16% of the sale price**, after a R90 marketing allowance ([Insaka](https://www.insaka.co.za/blog/print-on-demand-in-south-africa-local-vs-international-options)). Five levers move these numbers most. Absorbing a R59–R119 courier fee as free shipping cuts the tee profit pool by about 30–50% [EST], so delivery should stay a visible pass-through, kept low with locker and Pargo/Paxi options. Payment mix matters because EFT and Capitec Pay at 2% cost less than cards at 3.2% + R2 ([PayFast](https://payfast.io/fees)). VAT status matters while MerchMe is below the new R2.3m compulsory threshold: an unregistered MerchMe would keep about R33 more per tee [EST], but would lose input-VAT claims on build and equipment costs. Volume pricing on hoodie blanks has the largest single effect on cost. Finally, the managed Pro tier is where MerchMe can charge for work rather than squeeze creators.

## Break-even needs roughly 76 orders a day, which only the modelled upside case reaches

Every input below is an assumption the partners can change. All outputs are model calculations [EST]. The model excludes accessories, diaspora sales, B2B fulfilment, seasonality and corporate tax. It treats working capital as neutral, because fans pay upfront and creators are paid in arrears.

The model's central message is structural. Under a creator-fair split, MerchMe's margin is about R122 per unit at full scale (Stage C), or about R104 after variable costs [EST]. Against a full-scale team of about R309k a month, break-even needs about **2,275 orders a month (≈76 a day, ≈R19m of annual GMV)** [EST]. Under 75/25 the same team breaks even at about 1,470 orders a month (≈49 a day). That gap is the likely reason the 75/25 figure was chosen. But the creator market sets the split, not MerchMe's cost base. The base case reaches 1,598 orders a month by month 36, about 70% of break-even volume. Its hub opens in month 20, and it never reaches the Stage C trigger.

The sensitivities point to two conclusions. First, 75/25 does not rescue the business once its likely effect on creator sign-ups is priced in. Only if creators accepted a deal 2.4 times worse than Mercha's, at undiminished volumes, would it break even, and only in month 35. Second, no single cost cut gets the base case to break-even. Conversion and activity matter most. Only the combination of 25% of creators selling each month and five orders each crosses the line, in month 34. The decision therefore rests on a number nobody has measured in South Africa: how many followers of an up-and-coming creator actually buy. The only published range, 0.4–1.2% of followers a year, comes from a low-reliability vendor blog ([VoxBooster](https://voxbooster.com/pl/blog/merch-sales-creator-statistics-2026/)). Redbubble's long tail averaged an estimated A$112 per selling artist in FY22 [EST, from [ASX FY22](https://announcements.asx.com.au/asxpdf/20220817/pdf/45cxtlwtz0qbpl.pdf)]. Funding should be staged against that question rather than raised upfront. Cumulative need in every scenario is about **R0.25m to month 3** (pilot), **R1.0m to month 7** (MVP built) and **R1.5m to month 12** [EST]. After that the scenarios diverge: by month 24 the base case has used R3.3m and the upside R2.9m. African start-ups raised US$2.49bn in disclosed deals in the first nine months of 2026 ([TechCabal via African Leadership Magazine](https://www.facebook.com/africanleadershipmagazine/posts/according-to-techcabal-insights-african-startups-raised-249-billion-across-315-d/1535744481913489/)). But neither Mercha nor Nigeria's Poptrek shows any disclosed venture funding ([VC4A](https://vc4a.com/ventures/poptrek-inc/)). Early money will most likely come from the partners, angels or a strategic partner (a printer, label or festival), not venture capital.

The technology choice follows from how money flows. Shopify Payments is not offered in South Africa, so Shopify adds a third-party fee of 2% on the Basic plan ([Shopify ZA](https://www.shopify.com/za/pricing)). The open-source Mercur marketplace layer defaults to Stripe for payouts ([Medusa](https://medusajs.com/blog/get-started-with-mercur-2-0-on-cloud)). And no SA payment gateway offers drop-in marketplace payouts the way Stripe Connect does. Under the recommended principal model, though, MerchMe is legally one store with many creator storefronts, not a multi-vendor marketplace. A lean custom Next.js and Supabase build (Supabase Pro about US$25 a month, Vercel Pro US$20) handles that more simply than bending a marketplace engine to SA payment rails ([MakerKit](https://makerkit.dev/blog/saas/supabase-pricing); [Vercel](https://vercel.com/docs/plans/pro-plan/switching)).

The R650k estimate is about 1,100–1,300 hours at South African contract rates of around **R584 an hour for full-stack developers** ([Indeed ZA](https://za.indeed.com/cmp/Communicate-Recruitment/salaries/Full-Stack-Developer)). It is consistent with global marketplace MVP benchmarks of **US$30k–80k** ([LowCode Agency](https://www.lowcode.agency/blog/marketplace-mvp-development-cost)) [EST]. If one of the partners can code, it falls sharply. Mock-up APIs cost tens of dollars a month ([Dynamic Mockups](https://dynamicmockups.com/pricing/)). The main hidden build items are server-side generation of print files and a simple printer portal, which is needed because SA print partners take orders by email, CSV or store URL rather than an API ([TeePrint](https://teeprint.co.za/start-selling-shirts/); [OTC Printing](https://www.otcprinting.co.za/print-on-demand/)).

Payments must fix South Africa's biggest checkout problem. Retailers name **card declines (61.2%), shipping fees (51.7%) and forced account creation (43.8%)** as the top reasons for abandoned carts ([World Wide Worx 2026](https://www.mastercard.com/news/media/hwgncwio/online-retail-in-south-africa-2026.pdf)), and Apple Pay transactions succeed about 96% of the time against about 80% for cards ([Stitch](https://stitch.money/blog/report-the-state-of-consumer-payments-in-south-africa)). The launch checkout therefore needs guest checkout, cards, Apple Pay and Google Pay, Instant EFT and Capitec Pay. Paystack charges 2.9% + R1 on local cards and 2% on EFT, and supports split payments ([Paystack](https://support.paystack.com/en/articles/2130306)). PayFast covers Capitec Pay and SnapScan, but its split feature allows only one split per payment ([PayFast](https://payfast.io/?p=6563)). Creator payouts should come from MerchMe's own account through a payouts API. Peach Payments' real-time payouts arrive in about 90 seconds ([ITNewsAfrica](https://www.itnewsafrica.com/africa-by-region/southern-africa-africa-by-region/south-africa-peach-payments-announces-real-time-clearance-payouts/)). This avoids putting every small creator through gateway KYC. Because base-plus-markup fixes the creator's amount per SKU, payouts are simple to calculate, and they can be released weekly once the cooling-off period has passed.

The social layer is a link, not a native shop. Instagram allows up to five bio links, which open in its in-app browser ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/04/18/instagram-takes-on-linktree-and-others-with-support-for-up-to-5-links-in-bio/)). On TikTok, a clickable bio link needs **1,000+ followers or a registered business account** ([Stan](https://stan.store/blog/tiktok-link-bio-requirements-2026-guide/)), which shuts out part of the long tail. The product therefore needs lightweight pages for prepaid data users, per-channel links (bio, Story sticker, TikTok, WhatsApp, gig QR) and pre-filled WhatsApp sharing. About 35% of online shoppers have bought through a WhatsApp link ([World Wide Worx 2026](https://www.mastercard.com/news/media/hwgncwio/online-retail-in-south-africa-2026.pdf)).

Fulfilment starts with a panel of partners in two regions: Gauteng, plus OTC (1–3 day best case, white-label) or Lucani (3–5 days) in the Western Cape. Delivery runs through TCG Lockers at **R59–R69 locker-to-locker** and Pargo's **4,000+ pickup points**. Paxi (R59.95, slower) serves township and rural fans, for whom door delivery carries a **R100–R150 surcharge** ([CTNews](https://ctnews.co.za/cheapest-ways-to-send-a-parcel-from-cape-town-courier-lockers-paxi-and-pargo-compared/); [Growth Pulse Media](https://www.growthpulsemedia.co.za/courier-costs-for-ecommerce-south-africa/)). The hub brings quality control and margin in-house once volume justifies it. Power risk is currently low: there had been 476 consecutive days without load-shedding by September 2026 ([Eskom](https://www.eskom.co.za/eskom-fully-meets-2026-winter-demand-eaf-remains-at-its-highest-level-since-2020-67-79-and-diesel-expenditure-declines-by-r4-84-billion-or-81-64-to-deliver-energy-security/)). Even so, a R22k–R35k 5kVA inverter is cheap insurance in Gauteng ([Versofy](https://www.versofy.energy/blog/solar-power-with-battery-backup-price-and-performance)).

Nothing in the research shows the partners' individual skills, so the team split is a suggestion. One partner owns creators and growth: recruitment, managers, labels, festivals and Pro-tier sales. The other owns product and operations: the build, print partners, quality control, support and finance. Development is contracted until there is product-market fit.

## Selling as principal simplifies payments but concentrates legal liability

The core legal choice is whether MerchMe is the **principal seller**, selling its own goods and paying creators a royalty, or an **agent** selling on creators' behalf. Principal is the right choice. With a 75% platform share, or MerchMe controlling price, printing and fulfilment, agency treatment would be fragile for VAT under s54 ([Van Deventers](https://vandeventers.law/legal-articles/entryid/1211/section-54-of-the-vat-act-south-africa-explained)) [CONFIRM]. Being principal also keeps MerchMe outside the third-party payment provider rule. That rule requires PASA registration through a sponsoring bank for anyone who "accept[s] payments from multiple parties for your client" ([PASA](https://authorisation.pasa.org.za/so-and-tppp/tppp-registration)), and the SARB's draft activity-based framework is moving towards authorisation triggers linked to transaction volumes ([Webber Wentzel](https://www.webberwentzel.com/News/Pages/south-africas-payments-regulatory-framework-third-draft-of-authorisation-framework-published-for-comment.aspx)) [CONFIRM]. The trade-off is that MerchMe becomes the "supplier" for every consumer-law duty and the reproducer of every design.

IP is the risk most likely to cause real damage. The bootleg market for SA stars shows exactly what some uploaders will try to sell. The go thresholds match the base case [EST]. A creator with 20k followers who sells about 15–20 orders per drop, drops every few months and sells a trickle in between delivers roughly the four orders a month the base model assumes for an active creator. Later gates use the model's trajectories. At **Gate 2 (month 12)**, at least about 160 orders a month growing 15% month on month keeps the base plan alive. At least 400 orders a month justifies raising growth capital on the upside path. Below about 50 means stopping, or pivoting to Pro, events or B2B fulfilment. At **Gate 3 (month 24)**, the hub should be live and orders at or above about 730 a month. Otherwise the partners should cut back to a founder-run, outsourced business, or pursue a merger or partnership with a printer or with Mercha.

The next steps are concrete. In the first two weeks, the partners formally drop 75/25 for self-serve creators. They incorporate a Pty Ltd, register an Information Officer, and brief a lawyer on the payments, ECTA and IP opinions. They sign up to Mercha as a creator to confirm its current split, onboarding time and payout terms, and place a Mercha order to test delivery. In weeks 2–4, they get price lists and blank samples from OTC, Lucani, TeePrint, T-POD and a Johannesburg DTF bureau such as Blank Media, and wash-test the prints. They recruit the 15 pilot creators, run the split-offer test, and set up the Shopify, PayFast and Bob Go store. In weeks 5–10, they run the drops and collect the funnel data. In week 12, they score Gate 1 against the table above and update the scenario model with the measured conversion before committing any build money. In parallel, they should open exploratory conversations with one print partner about a revenue-share "self-serve layer" and with one festival (Cotton Fest already sells merch through its bio link ([Cotton Fest](https://www.facebook.com/CottonFestJHB/posts/cottoneaters-cottonfest-merch-is-officially-available-onlinefollow-the-link-in-o/788730686036343/))) about event pop-ups, because both bring volume without adding fixed cost.

## Conclusion

The 75/25 split hides the real problem. It looks like a pricing choice, but it is a symptom of thin unit economics: an SA print-on-demand tee leaves about R215 to divide, and a platform needs about three-quarters of that to cover a team at modest volume. The market will not grant that share while Mercha offers creators the reverse. So MerchMe's viability does not depend on finding a split that sounds fair. It depends on whether a creator-fair version can reach about 76 orders a day. On current assumptions that takes upside-level conversion, cheaper in-house production, or extra volume from events, Pro clients and B2B fulfilment. The most valuable thing MerchMe could build may turn out to be infrastructure, not a consumer brand: South Africa still lacks a Printful-style automated print-and-ship layer with local payment and courier rails, and that layer serves Mercha, Shopify sellers and creators alike.

The decision is also cheap to make well. The one number that decides the case, follower-to-buyer conversion among emerging SA creators, has never been published. Measuring it costs about R250k [EST], against a downside of R5m or more if the base case is built and stalls [EST]. If the pilot clears its gates, MerchMe enters with real data that Mercha may not have. If it fails, the partners have avoided a three-year cash drain, and they still hold supplier relationships and creator contacts that a fulfilment or events business could use.
